Before you begin

Equipment you need

  • Digital kitchen scales (1 g accuracy)
  • Kitchen bowl, minimum 3 litre capacity
  • Dough whisk or sturdy wooden spoon
  • A chopstick
  • Bowl scraper (semi-circular)
  • Clean glass or plastic jar — your levain jar (min 600 ml)
  • Razor blade (lame) or sharp knife
  • A banneton, bowl or basket · lint-free cloth if banneton is not cloth-lined
  • A dutch oven, baking stone with lid, or other suitable baking dish
  • Bakers couche (proofing cloth) or a clean floured pillow case
  • Large flat baking tray
  • Baking paper · Cling wrap or shower cap · Oven-proof gloves · Cooling rack
Nice to have

In order of importance: a dough whisk, bowl scraper, banneton and dutch oven (loaves) or couche (baguettes). I also have accurate digital tea scales (0.01 g accuracy) for measuring salt precisely. All can be bought on Amazon for well under $100 in total.

A bit of theory

Baker's percentages

Most ingredients are expressed as a percentage of the total flour in the bread. Hydration refers to water as a percentage of total flour. Salt is usually 2% and mix-ins around 15%.

About hydration

Your starter is kept at 100% hydration — always feed with equal parts flour and water.

A bit of science

Bread flour contains starch and proteins. Water activates the proteins into gluten — an elastic network that traps gas. Yeasts in the starter produce CO₂ (rise), bacteria produce lactic acid (sour taste and preservation).

Ingredients

Flour

I recommend Wallaby brand white bread flour — sold in 5 kg bags in most supermarkets (~$14). The higher protein content compared to plain flour gives a better crumb structure.

Step 1 · ~24 hours before baking

The pre-ferment

Not enough starter?

Starter care

Keep starter in a clean glass jar in the fridge. Whenever you bake, replace what you use by mixing equal parts flour and water back into the jar. Feed weekly if baking less than once a week — discard a tablespoon and mix in 20 g each of flour and water.

For absences over two weeks, make a stiff dry levain (twice as much flour as water), freeze it. To revive, leave at room temperature until thawed then feed with equal parts flour and water. It should be active within 24 hours.

Step 2 · ~12–14 hours before baking

Mixing the dough

Add ingredients to your bowl in this order:

  1. Measure out of levain into your mixing bowl
  2. Add water
  3. Add salt
  4. Mix for 15 seconds using a chopstick, dough whisk or fork
  5. Add flour
  6. Mix with dough whisk or wooden spoon for 45–60 seconds
  7. Wet your hands and bowl scraper. Cut through any dry patches and combine into a homogenous mass
  8. Cover with food wrap or towel and leave on benchtop
No kneading required

Gluten structures develop on their own. Keep the dough covered throughout to prevent drying out.

Optional — autolyse

Mix flour and water and rest 30–60 minutes before adding levain and salt. Improves texture and reduces mixing time.

Step 3 · During the 1.5–2 hours after mixing

Bulk fermentation

Adding mix-ins

Stretch and fold

▶ Watch: Stretch & fold technique

Once every 30–45 minutes, for a total of 3 sets:

  1. Wet your hands and bowl scraper
  2. Pinch the side of the dough, stretch away from the mass, fold to the opposite side
  3. Rotate bowl 90° and repeat — 4 folds in total
  4. Cover the bowl again

Timing is flexible. You can refrigerate the bowl for 8–10 hours and take it out ~2 hours before shaping.

Step 4 · After last stretch and fold

Shaping the dough

Leave covered for another 1.5–2 hours. The dough should have risen ~50–75% and feel airy. Lightly flour a clean benchtop, your hands and the scraper.

Divide and pre-shape

▶ Watch: Divide & pre-shape

Gently take a quarter of the dough, stretch it slightly and fold to the opposite side. Repeat three more times to form a rough ball. Turn so the floured side is on top. Dust lightly and rest 15–20 minutes.

Final shape

▶ Watch: Final shaping
  1. Flour hands and scraper. Dust banneton with rice flour
  2. Flip dough over. Fold two opposite sides into the middle to create a rectangular shape
  3. Roll up like rolling carpet to create a cylinder
  4. Drag the cylinder across the benchtop to build surface tension
  5. Dust with rice flour and flip into banneton. Cover and refrigerate 4–12 hours
Shaping tips

Use the benchtop's friction to build tension in the outer skin. Think sticky side (bottom of dough contacting the benchtop) and smooth side (becomes the top of the loaf — this goes on the bottom in the banneton). You can also stitch the sticky side together in the banneton for extra tension.

Step 5 · Baking day

Baking the loaf

  1. At least 30 minutes before baking, place baking dish and lid in oven (lid open) and preheat to maximum (up to 250°C)
  2. 5 minutes before baking, take banneton from fridge. Cut baking paper to size for a cradle
  3. Sprinkle dough with rice flour. Place paper cradle over banneton, flip, remove banneton
  4. Wet razor blade. Score — roughly 5 mm deep at 45° to the dough, parallel to the long axis
  5. Lower loaf into hot baking dish using paper as a cradle. Close lid
  6. Bake at maximum heat for 20 minutes with lid on
  7. Remove lid (and paper if possible). Reduce to 220°C. Bake further 20–25 minutes until deep golden
  8. Cool on rack for at least 1 hour before slicing
Why the lid matters

Steam is the key — the loaf rises before the crust sets. The lid traps steam from the dough itself. Don't cut for at least an hour — escaping steam makes the crumb gummy.

Good luck! 🍞

Step 4 · After last stretch and fold

Shaping baguettes

Leave covered 1.5–2 hours after the last fold. Dough should have risen ~50–75%. Lightly flour a clean benchtop, your hands and the scraper.

Divide and pre-shape

▶ Watch: Divide & pre-shape

Use your scraper (and scales for accuracy) to divide the dough into equal portions. Shape each into a rough cylinder by folding the sides in. Dust with flour and rest 15–20 minutes.

Final shape

  1. Dust couche generously with rice flour or semolina. Place on a large tray and create wave-like folds — baguettes will proof in these
  2. Flip cylinder over. Flatten into a rectangle. Fold the long side to two-thirds and seal with flat hand. Fold remaining third to make a cigar shape
  3. Flour hands well. From the middle, roll baguette back and forth, increasing pressure toward the ends to elongate
  4. Dust generously with semolina or rice flour and place in a fold of the couche
  5. Cover with kitchen towel and refrigerate 4–12 hours. Avoid very long proofs — baguettes are more delicate than loaves
Shaping tips

Use the benchtop's friction to build tension in the outer skin. Think sticky side (bottom of dough contacting the benchtop) and smooth side (becomes the top of the baguette — this goes on the bottom in the couche). Baguettes are more forgiving in shape but less forgiving with over-proofing.

Step 5 · Baking day

Baking baguettes

  1. Preheat oven to maximum (up to 250°C) at least 30 minutes before baking. Place a heavy pan (cast iron ideal) on the oven floor
  2. 5 minutes before baking, take couche from fridge
  3. Cover tray with baking paper, sprinkle with rice flour or semolina
  4. Carefully transfer baguettes to tray — maximum 3 per standard tray
  5. Score each baguette — roughly 5 mm deep at an almost horizontal angle, slightly off-centre, overlapping by about a third
  6. Just before loading, pour boiling water over a towel in the hot pan to create steam. Optionally mist baguettes lightly with water
  7. Bake at maximum heat with steam for at least 12 minutes
  8. Vent or remove steam. Reduce to 220°C and bake a further 10–15 minutes until deep golden
  9. Cool on rack for at least 40 minutes before cutting
Steam is essential for baguettes

Without steam the crust sets too early and you lose the characteristic open crumb and thin crackly crust. Keep steam going for the first 12–15 minutes.

Good luck! 🥖
